Grade 5–6 math · Factors & Multiples

Break it, or build it?

"Common factor" and "common multiple" sound like two hard words to memorize. Really, they answer two very different kinds of everyday questions. Start with this pair:

Factor = break apart

Split a number evenly. How many friends can share 12 cookies fairly?

A factor is never bigger than the number itself.

Multiple = build up

Stack the same amount again and again. Buns come 8 to a pack. How many do you get with more packs?

A multiple is never smaller than the number itself.

Stop 2 · Common multiples

Two frogs. When do they land on the same stone?

Blue frog A jumps 4 spaces at a time. Orange frog B jumps 6. Both start at 0. The numbers each frog lands on are its multiples. The numbers both frogs land on are common multiples.

Hot dogs and buns

Hot dogs come 10 to a pack. Buns come 8 to a pack. To end up with exactly one bun per hot dog, what's the smallest amount? The 10-frog and the 8-frog first meet at 40: buy 4 packs of hot dogs and 5 packs of buns.

Two blinking lights

One light blinks every 6 seconds, another every 9 seconds. They just blinked together. They'll blink together again in 18 seconds.

The big idea: a common multiple is when two rhythms line up again. When a question asks "when is the next time together?" or "what's the least amount that comes out even?", you want the least common multiple (LCM). It's always at least as big as the larger number, because you're building up.

Goodie bags

18 pencils and 24 erasers go into identical goodie bags, with nothing left over. What's the most bags you can make? The greatest common factor of 18 and 24 is 6: 6 bags, each with 3 pencils and 4 erasers.

Floor tiles

A 12 × 18 floor is covered with equal square tiles, with no cutting. What's the biggest tile? 6 × 6, which fits exactly 2 × 3 tiles.

The big idea: a common factor is one ruler that measures both things exactly. When a question asks "what's the most groups?" or "what's the longest/biggest piece?", you want the greatest common factor (GCF). It's never bigger than the smaller number, because you're breaking apart.

Stop 4 · What's underneath

Every number is built from prime blocks

Primes (2, 3, 5, 7, …) are the smallest blocks; they can't be broken apart. Every number can be written as prime blocks multiplied together, and there's only one way to do it. Once you see this, common factors and common multiples turn out to be two sides of the same idea.

The big idea: common factors are made of the blocks both numbers share, so the GCF uses every shared block. A common multiple must hold all the blocks of both numbers, so the LCM takes everything either one needs, and not one block more. Shared blocks get counted only once, which leads to a neat secret: GCF × LCM = A × B.

Stop 5 · What they're really for

Why learn this at all?

At school, the first place they show up is fractions:

What you're doingWhich oneExample
Simplifying a fraction to lowest termsGCF12/18 → divide top and bottom by 6 → 2/3
Adding fractions with different denominatorsLCM1/4 + 1/6 → make both denominators 12 → 3/12 + 2/12 = 5/12

In everyday life, they answer two kinds of questions:

Breaking apart → common factor

Grouping, packing bags, cutting material, tiling, lining up in rows. Look for: the most groups, the biggest piece, no waste.

Building up → common multiple

Schedules, buses, blinking lights, buying things that come in packs. Look for: next time together, the least amount, coming out even.

The deepest reason: you're learning to find a shared unit. Two things have different lengths or different rhythms, and you need one amount that fits both exactly. A woodworker finding a length that divides both boards, a musician finding the beat where two rhythms meet, a student finding a common denominator: it's the same idea every time.

For parents

Three tips for using this page together

  • Don't rush to teach the method. Before every problem, ask: "Are we breaking it apart, or building it up?" Once that's right, the arithmetic is the easy part.
  • In Stop 1, have your child drag the slider to 7, 11 and 13, and ask why those only make one long row. Let them explain what "prime" means in their own words.
  • Try it with real things: a box of blocks, two pieces of string, scrap wood. Splitting something by hand once sticks better than ten worksheet problems.
Other names you may see: the GCF is also called the GCD (greatest common divisor) or HCF (highest common factor). LCM stands for least common multiple. A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself.
